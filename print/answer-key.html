Major Revisions: flaw key

Each study's planted flaws, the verdict each one earns, and the request that catches it with its reporting-guideline item. A manuscript's verdict is that of its worst flaw.

S1 · Back on track (Randomised trial, CONSORT 2025)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Question
MR-1Q-F		Switched after registration: Registered with pain at 12 weeks as primary outcome. The paper makes function primary and never says why.Major revisionsRegistration (CONSORT 2025 item 2–3)Switching the primary outcome after seeing the data lets a chance finding pass as the main result. Fixable: report the registered outcome as primary and explain the change. Goldacre et al
Method
MR-1M-F		Predictable allocation: Patients seen on Mondays and Wednesdays got exercise; patients seen on other days got usual care.RejectRandomisation (CONSORT 2025 item 17a–19)Staff could foresee each allocation and steer who was enrolled on which day, so the groups may differ from the start. No reanalysis can undo that. Schulz et al
Method
MR-1M-F2		Intervention not described: Described only as 'supervised exercise, twice a week'; the exercises, intensity and progression are not given.Minor revisionsIntervention details (CONSORT 2025 item 13)This gap doesn't bias the comparison, but nobody can repeat the programme or offer it to patients. Minor: describe it in full (TIDieR), for example in a supplement. Hoffmann et al
Sample
MR-1S-F		Hand-picked participants: Physiotherapists enrolled only patients they judged 'motivated to exercise'; no written criteria.Minor revisionsRecruitment (CONSORT 2025 item 12a)Randomisation still balances the groups, so the comparison is fair, but the result may not apply to typical patients. Minor: report it and narrow the claims. Rothwell (2005)
Sample
MR-1S-F2		Flow not reported: No flow diagram: 240 were randomised, but losses are not given per arm or with reasons.Minor revisionsFlow diagram (CONSORT 2025 item 22a–b)Few were lost (14 of 240, 6%) and the missing-data analysis covers them, but readers need to see who left each arm and why. Minor: add the CONSORT flow diagram. Egger et al
Measurement
MR-1M-F		Unblinded assessors: Each patient's own physiotherapist scored the test, knowing who had the exercise programme.RejectBlinding (CONSORT 2025 item 20a–b)Assessors who know the groups tend to see the effect they expect. A 12-week performance test can't be repeated blind afterwards, so the main result can't be rescued. Hróbjartsson et al
Analysis
MR-1A-F		Per-protocol only: Only patients who attended at least 80% of sessions were analysed: 61 of 120 in the exercise arm.Major revisionsAnalysis set (CONSORT 2025 item 21b, 26)Dropping non-adherers breaks randomisation: people who keep exercising differ from those who stop. Fixable: reanalyse everyone as randomised (intention to treat). Hollis & Campbell (1999)

S2 · Late-night scrolling (Cohort study, STROBE)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Question
MR-2Q-F		HARKing: The introduction predicts an effect in girls only. The proposal predicted it for everyone; only girls showed it.Major revisionsPrespecified aims (STROBE item 3)A hypothesis written after the results makes a chance subgroup finding look like a confirmed prediction. Fixable: restore the original hypothesis and label the girls-only result exploratory. Kerr (1998)
Method
MR-2M-F		Exposure after outcome: Both were asked at 15: pupils recalled how much they had used social media two years earlier.RejectSetting and dates (STROBE item 5)Use at 13 is recalled after the sleep problems began, so the timing that made it a prediction is gone. Only data collected at 13 could fix this. Hill (1965)
Sample
MR-2S-F		Selective loss to follow-up: Heavy users were twice as likely to be lost by 15; leavers and stayers are never compared.Major revisionsFlow diagram (STROBE item 13a–c)If the heavy users who left slept differently, the estimate is biased. Fixable: compare leavers with stayers and correct with weighting or imputation. Kristman et al
Sample
MR-2S-F2		Sample size not explained: The paper doesn't say how the cohort size was set, or what precision it gives the main estimate.Minor revisionsSample size (STROBE item 10)The parent cohort fixed the size, so the data stand; readers need only that sentence and the estimate's confidence interval. Minor: explain how the size was set. Charles et al
Measurement
MR-2M-F		Unvalidated measure: One yes/no item written for this study, 'Do you often sleep badly?', with no validation.Major revisionsMeasures (STROBE item 7–8)One home-made yes/no item can't show it measures sleep problems. Fixable in part: use the cohort's fuller sleep measures, or validate the item in a subsample, and temper the claims. Flake & Fried (2020)
Analysis
MR-2A-F		p-hacking: Use was split at 3 hours after trying cut-offs from 1 to 5; only the one with p < 0.05 is reported.Major revisionsSensitivity analysis (STROBE item 12e)Trying many cut-offs and reporting the one that works inflates false positives. Fixable: analyse hours as a continuous measure, or show every cut-off. Simmons et al
Analysis
MR-2A-F2		No confidence interval: Adjusted for sleep, mood and income at 13, but only the adjusted odds ratio and a p-value are given.Minor revisionsConfounder adjustment (STROBE item 12a, 16a)A p-value says nothing about how large or how precise the effect is, and without the unadjusted estimate readers can't see what adjustment changed. Minor: report both, with 95% confidence intervals. Gardner & Altman (1986)

S3 · Sleeping pills and hip fracture (Case-control study, STROBE)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Question
MR-3Q-F		p-hacking: Nine sleep medicines were tested. The headline is the one significant drug, with no correction for nine tests.Major revisionsPrespecified aims (STROBE item 3)With nine tests, one 'significant' result is likely by chance alone. Fixable: report all nine, correct for multiple testing, and call the single-drug finding exploratory. Simmons et al
Method
MR-3M-F		Exposure after outcome: Drug use counted from 6 months before to 1 month after the fracture, when many start sleeping pills in hospital.Major revisionsSetting and dates (STROBE item 5)Pills started because of the fracture are counted as its cause. Fixable: rerun the analysis with use before the fracture date only. Hill (1965)
Sample
MR-3S-F		Unsuitable controls: Controls were volunteers recruited at senior fitness clubs.RejectRecruitment (STROBE item 6a)Fitness-club members take fewer sleeping pills and fall less than the population the cases came from, so the comparison is biased from the start. New controls are needed. Wacholder et al
Sample
MR-3S-F2		Flow not reported: Participation given only overall ('about 80%'), not for cases and controls separately; no reasons for refusal.Minor revisionsFlow diagram (STROBE item 13a–c)Bias arises if taking part depends on pill use differently in cases and controls; per-group rates and reasons are the first check. Minor: report them per group; here they are similar (81% and 76%). Vandenbroucke et al
Measurement
MR-3M-F		Recall bias: Use recalled in interviews: cases on the ward days after their fracture, controls by phone.Major revisionsMeasures (STROBE item 7–8)Cases search their memory harder, in a different setting, so their use is over-reported. Fixable in part: check the answers against pharmacy records. Coughlin (1990)
Analysis
MR-3A-F		Uncontrolled confounding: Adjusted for age and sex only. Dementia and frailty, which lead to both pills and falls, are ignored.Major revisionsConfounder adjustment (STROBE item 12a, 16a)Frail people get more sleeping pills and fall more, so part of the 'drug effect' may be frailty. Fixable if the data hold these factors: adjust for them and show both estimates. Kyriacou & Lewis (2016)
Analysis
MR-3A-F2		Checks not shown: 'Sensitivity analyses confirmed the results', the paper says, without saying which analyses or showing them.Minor revisionsSensitivity analysis (STROBE item 12e)Readers can't tell what was checked, or how closely it agreed. Minor: describe each sensitivity analysis and show its results, for example in a supplement. Thabane et al

S4 · Burnout in the doctorate (Cross-sectional survey, STROBE)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Question
MR-4Q-F		Overclaiming: The aim is descriptive, yet the abstract concludes that poor supervision causes burnout.Minor revisionsPrespecified aims (STROBE item 3)A one-off survey can't show which came first, burnout or poor supervision. Minor: rewrite the conclusion as an association. Boutron & Ravaud (2018)
Method
MR-4M-F		Missing detail: Dates given only as 'spring 2025'; when the survey opened and closed, and what fell in that window, isn't said.Minor revisionsSetting and dates (STROBE item 5)Answers can shift with the academic calendar (exams, teaching, funding rounds), so readers need the exact window. Minor: give the opening and closing dates and what fell within them. Glasziou et al
Sample
MR-4S-F		Self-selected sample: The link was shared on a PhD well-being forum and on social media; anyone could answer.RejectRecruitment (STROBE item 6a)Whether people answer depends on how much burnout matters to them, so the prevalence can't be generalised. A new, defined sample is needed. Hernán et al
Measurement
MR-4M-F		Unvalidated measure: Five items written by the authors; 'burnout' means scoring above the midpoint; no validation.Major revisionsMeasures (STROBE item 7–8)A prevalence is only as good as the measure and its cut-off. Fixable only in part: give validity evidence, or report scores instead of a prevalence. Flake & Fried (2020)
Analysis
MR-4A-F		Missing data ignored: The 22% who skipped any item were dropped; skipping was commonest among those reporting long hours.Major revisionsMissing data (STROBE item 12c, 14b)If the students under most pressure skipped items, dropping them biases the prevalence. Fixable: multiple imputation, and compare completers with non-completers. Sterne et al
Analysis
MR-4A-F2		Checks not shown: Weighting by faculty and sex 'made no difference', the paper says, but the weighted estimates are not shown.Minor revisionsSensitivity analysis (STROBE item 12e)Readers can't see how big the difference was. Minor: show the weighted prevalence beside the unweighted one. Thabane et al

S5 · Moral distress in intensive care (Interview study, COREQ)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Method
MR-5M-F		Interviewer with power: Every interview was done by the unit's head nurse, who also writes the nurses' appraisals.RejectReflexivity (COREQ item 1–8)Nurses are unlikely to speak freely to the person who appraises them, so the data themselves are compromised. New interviews are needed. Berger (2015)
Sample
MR-5S-F		Saturation not reached: Data collection stopped at 14 because the project ended. Saturation is claimed but not shown.Major revisionsData saturation (COREQ item 22)Saturation means new data stop adding insight, not that time ran out. Fixable: show how completeness was judged, or add interviews. Saunders et al
Sample
MR-5S-F2		Missing detail: Purposive sampling is named, but not how the nurses were approached, or by whom.Minor revisionsRecruitment (COREQ item 10–11)Readers can't judge whether the nurses felt free to say no. Minor: say who invited the nurses, and how. Glasziou et al
Measurement
MR-5M-F		Single coder, no audit trail: One researcher coded every transcript. There is no coding tree and no second reader.Major revisionsCoding tree (COREQ item 24–25)With one coder and no audit trail, readers can't judge how the codes came from the data. Fixable: a second coder, the coding tree and an audit trail. O'Connor & Joffe (2020)
Measurement
MR-5M-F2		Missing detail: The guide's topics are listed, but its questions and prompts aren't given, and piloting isn't mentioned.Minor revisionsMeasures (COREQ item 17)Readers can't see what the nurses were asked or how the questions framed their answers. Minor: give the guide in full and say whether it was piloted. Glasziou et al
Analysis
MR-5A-F		Themes = interview questions: The four themes are the four headings of the interview guide, although the paper says they came from the data.Major revisionsTheme derivation (COREQ item 26)Using the interview questions as themes summarises the guide, not the data; Braun & Clarke (2006) name it a pitfall. Fixable: redo the analysis. Braun & Clarke (2006)

S6 · A scan for appendicitis (Diagnostic accuracy study, STARD 2015)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Question
MR-6Q-F		Switched after registration: Registered for patients of all ages. The paper reports children only and never mentions the adults.Major revisionsRegistration (STARD 2015 item 28–29)Leaving out a registered group can hide results that didn't suit. Fixable: report the whole registered population, or explain why not. Goldacre et al
Question
MR-6Q-F2		No prespecified target: The aim is 'to assess accuracy'; no minimum acceptable sensitivity was set in advance.Minor revisionsPrespecified aims (STARD 2015 item 4)Without a target, whether 97% is good enough is judged after the fact, but the estimate and its interval still stand. Minor: state that no target was set, and judge the lower confidence limit against what clinicians need. Korevaar et al
Method
MR-6M-F		Partial verification: Only children with a positive scan had surgery and histology. Negatives went home without follow-up.Major revisionsReference standard (STARD 2015 item 10a–11)Missed cases among the negatives are never found, so sensitivity looks too high. Fixable only if follow-up can still be obtained for the negatives. Begg & Greenes (1983)
Sample
MR-6S-F		Spectrum bias: Children with surgically confirmed appendicitis, compared with healthy children from a school check-up.RejectRecruitment (STARD 2015 item 6–7, 9)Comparing clear cases with healthy children leaves out the hard, look-alike cases, so accuracy is inflated. A new, clinically realistic sample is needed. Lijmer et al
Sample
MR-6S-F2		Flow not reported: No flow diagram: 398 children were scanned, but not how many were eligible, or why some lack a reference standard.Minor revisionsFlow diagram (STARD 2015 item 19)Readers can't tell who was never scanned or never verified, or whether they differ. Minor: add a STARD flow diagram with the reason for each child lost. Cohen et al
Measurement
MR-6M-F		Unblinded test reading: Stored scans were read after surgery, by doctors who knew the histology result.Major revisionsBlinding (STARD 2015 item 13a–b)Readers who know the answer read the scan in its light, which inflates accuracy. Fixable: re-read the stored scans blind. Whiting et al
Analysis
MR-6A-F		Data-driven cut-off: The 6 mm cut-off was picked after the data, as the one giving the best accuracy.Major revisionsTest cut-offs (STARD 2015 item 12a–b)A cut-off tuned on the same data flatters accuracy. Fixable: use a prespecified cut-off, or validate this one in new data. Leeflang et al

S7 · Mindfulness apps for anxiety (Systematic review and meta-analysis, PRISMA 2020)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Question
MR-7Q-F		Switched after registration: PROSPERO lists depression as the primary outcome. The review leads with anxiety and doesn't say why.Major revisionsRegistration (PRISMA 2020 item 24a–b)Choosing the headline outcome after seeing the results lets the best-looking one win. Fixable: report the registered outcome first and explain the change. Goldacre et al
Method
MR-7M-F		Narrow search: PubMed only, English only, no trial registers; the search string isn't given.Major revisionsSearch strategy (PRISMA 2020 item 6–7)One database misses many trials, and the missed ones are not a random set. Fixable: redo the search across several sources. Bramer et al
Sample
MR-7S-F		Unpublished studies left out: Only trials published in journals were eligible; register results and theses were excluded.Major revisionsRecruitment (PRISMA 2020 item 5)Unpublished trials more often find no effect, so leaving them out inflates the pooled effect. Fixable: widen eligibility and look for them. Hopewell et al
Sample
MR-7S-F2		Flow not reported: Only the 23 included trials are listed: no flow diagram, no count of records screened, no reasons for exclusion.Minor revisionsFlow diagram (PRISMA 2020 item 16a)Readers can't check the selection or spot trials wrongly left out. Minor: add a PRISMA flow diagram with a reason for each full text excluded. Page et al
Measurement
MR-7M-F		No risk-of-bias assessment: No risk-of-bias tool was used; trials were called 'high quality' because they were peer-reviewed.Major revisionsRisk-of-bias assessment (PRISMA 2020 item 11, 18)Without judging each trial's bias, nobody can tell whether the effect rests on weak trials. Fixable: assess with RoB 2 and run a sensitivity analysis by risk of bias. Sterne et al
Analysis
MR-7A-F		Unexplored heterogeneity: I² = 89%, yet one pooled effect is presented as the answer, and the differences are not explored.Major revisionsHeterogeneity (PRISMA 2020 item 13d–e)When trials disagree this much, one average may describe none of them. Fixable: explore the causes (subgroups, meta-regression) and temper the summary. Higgins & Thompson (2002)
Analysis
MR-7A-F2		Overclaiming: Funnel plot and Egger's test show no clear asymmetry, so the review concludes there is 'no publication bias'.Minor revisionsReporting bias (PRISMA 2020 item 14, 21)These tests can miss bias, and a symmetrical funnel can't prove that no studies are missing. Minor: reword as 'no clear evidence of small-study effects'. Boutron & Ravaud (2018)

S8 · A drug for stroke mice (Animal experiment, ARRIVE 2.0)

CardPlanted flawVerdictCaught byWhy it matters
Question
MR-8Q-F		Switched after registration: Registered with a 24-hour behaviour score as primary. The paper reports infarct volume and omits the behaviour score.Major revisionsRegistration (ARRIVE 2.0 item 19)Dropping the registered outcome hides a result that may not have suited. Fixable: report the behaviour score and explain the switch. Goldacre et al
Method
MR-8M-F		Predictable allocation: The first mice taken out of each cage got NR-4; the rest got vehicle.RejectRandomisation (ARRIVE 2.0 item 4a)The mice caught first can differ (calmer, slower, smaller), so the groups may differ before treatment. The experiment has to be repeated with random allocation. Schulz et al
Method
MR-8M-F2		Intervention not described: Dose and timing are given; the route, the vehicle and the anaesthesia are not described.Minor revisionsIntervention details (ARRIVE 2.0 item 9a–d)Readers can't check that both groups were handled alike, and nobody can repeat the experiment exactly. Minor: report route, vehicle and anaesthesia, as ARRIVE item 9 asks. Hoffmann et al
Sample
MR-8S-F		Unreported exclusions: Five NR-4 mice died or were excluded; they were replaced and are not mentioned.Major revisionsFlow diagram (ARRIVE 2.0 item 3b–c)Replacing animals that died hides harm and can leave only the mice that responded. Fixable: report every animal and analyse with the losses included. Holman et al
Sample
MR-8S-F2		Sample size not explained: Twelve per group, 'as is usual in the field'; no sample size calculation is reported.Minor revisionsSample size (ARRIVE 2.0 item 2b)Small studies that reach significance tend to overstate the effect, and readers can't tell what this one was designed to detect. Minor: say how 12 was chosen, and give the 40% with its 95% confidence interval. Charles et al
Measurement
MR-8M-F		Unblinded assessors: The researcher who dosed the mice also measured the slices, knowing each group.Major revisionsBlinding (ARRIVE 2.0 item 5)Tracing infarct edges takes judgement that expectations can sway. Fixable here, unlike in a live test: the stored slices can be re-measured blind. Hróbjartsson et al
Analysis
MR-8A-F		Pseudoreplication: Each brain slice counted as a separate observation: 120 slices instead of 24 mice.Major revisionsAnalysis set (ARRIVE 2.0 item 2a, 3c)Slices from one mouse are not independent, so the p-value is far too small. Fixable: reanalyse with the mouse as the unit. Lazic (2010)
